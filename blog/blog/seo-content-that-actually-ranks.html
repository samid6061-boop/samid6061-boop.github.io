SEO content that actually ranks: the structure I use on every article

Most SEO advice stops at "do keyword research." The part that actually moves rankings is what you do after — how the piece is structured before you write a single sentence of the draft. Here's the process I run on client work.

1. Start with the question, not the keyword

A keyword tells you what people type. It doesn't tell you what they actually want when they type it. Before writing anything, I search the target term myself and read the top 5 results with one question in mind: what job is this person trying to finish?

A search for "best CRM for small business" is a comparison job — the reader wants a shortlist, not a 3,000-word history of customer relationship management. A search for "how does a CRM work" is an explainer job. Matching the content type to the intent matters more than the keyword density ever will.

2. Outline against the gap, not the topic

I read every ranking page for the target term and note what they all cover — that's the baseline you need just to compete. Then I look for what none of them cover well: a missing example, an outdated number, a step nobody explains clearly. That gap becomes the reason my version deserves to outrank theirs. An article that just repeats what's already ranking has no reason to move above it.

3. Structure for skimmers first, readers second

Most visitors decide within seconds whether to keep reading. The structure has to work even if someone only reads headers:

  • One clear H1 that states the topic in plain language, not a clever headline.
  • H2s as promises — each one previews exactly what the section delivers, so a skimmer can jump straight to the part they need.
  • Short paragraphs — 2–4 sentences. Long blocks get skipped, not read.
  • An early answer — if the query has a direct answer, give it in the first 100 words. Depth comes after, not instead of.

4. Write the draft fast, edit it slow

I write the first draft without stopping to polish — the goal is getting the structure filled in while the research is fresh. The actual SEO work happens in the edit pass: cutting filler words, replacing vague claims with specific numbers or examples, and checking that every H2 still matches what the section actually delivers.

5. Optimize on-page basics — but don't stop there

Title tag and meta description written for the click, not just the crawler. Target term in the H1 and at least one H2, used naturally. Internal links to relevant pages on the same site. Alt text on images that describes what's actually in them. None of this is exotic — but skipping it is still the most common reason a genuinely good article underperforms.

6. Plan for freshness before you publish

Search engines favor content that stays current. Before publishing, I note which facts, prices, or statistics in the piece are likely to go stale, and set a rough date to revisit. An article updated twice a year consistently outperforms one written once and abandoned — even if the original was better written.

None of this is complicated, but doing all six steps on every single article — instead of skipping to the writing — is what separates content that ranks from content that just exists.
