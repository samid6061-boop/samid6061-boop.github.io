I analyzed 79 Shorts in 28 days: three videos got 64% of my views

I exported 28 days of YouTube Studio data for my sports channel, The Pavilion Report, and looked at every video in it. The headline is uncomfortable and useful: most of my videos did almost nothing, and three did almost everything. Here is the data, what I think it shows, and what it can't prove.

The numbers at a glance

The export lists 491 videos with views in the period. 79 of them were published inside the window, and together they earned 462,720 views, about 97% of the total. The other 412 older videos earned 14,059 views between them, and the typical one earned 2. The median new video earned 1,171 views.

My reading: this is a news-cycle channel. Rankings and tables are useful for a few days and then stop being searched. I don't have an evergreen back catalogue, and the numbers say so plainly.

The first video followed a real event. On 25 October in Lahore, Pakistan were 154 for 9 chasing 242 against England, and Shadab Khan's unbeaten 113 and an 89-run last-wicket stand with Haris Rauf won it by one wicket. It was reported as a record last-wicket stand in a successful ODI chase (see the reports by Al Jazeera and Outlook India). I posted the rankings angle the next day.

So there isn't one "viral shape" I can plan around, and I'd be wary of anyone who says there is.

What the pattern suggests

1. The hits changed a result or a ranking. My own explanation for the 100K+ videos is that they covered something with consequences: Pakistan's win, and what it did to Pakistan's place in the international rankings. The titles carry that: a specific number, a named team or player, and something at stake ("0.1 points from #3"). I can't prove that is why people watched. It is what the three biggest videos have in common.

2. A news day is not the same as my audience's news. On 18 November the IPL 2027 teams published their retained and released players, so I uploaded 12 videos that day, 8 of them IPL lists. Across 17 to 19 November, my 14 IPL list videos earned 3,716 views in total, with a median of about 154. The 12 ICC rankings and table videos I published on the same three days had a median of about 970. One of them, on Australia dropping a rating point in the ODI rankings, earned 13,406 views. It looks as if people come to my channel for rankings and tables with a twist, not for team-by-team announcements. That's one episode, so I'm treating it as a lead.

3. People kept watching the winners. I worked out roughly how much of each Short people watched (watch time divided by views, divided by video length). For the 42 videos old enough to compare fairly, the top quarter by views averaged about 63% watched, and the bottom quarter about 46%. Retention and views move together, though I can't tell which pushes which.

4. Generic hype didn't help. Some of my weakest videos had the loudest titles, with emoji and exclamation marks but nothing at stake. My best ones were quieter and more specific. Among the 42 comparable videos, the ones with emoji had a lower median (about 1,400) than those without (about 1,650), which is a small gap on a small sample.

5. Thumbnails barely matter here. The channel had about 121,000 thumbnail impressions against 477,000 views, so for every 100 views there were roughly 25 impressions. Most views don't start with a thumbnail click, they come from the Shorts feed. That is my inference from those two totals, and it is why I don't lean on click-through rate for Shorts.

What this doesn't prove

  • It's 28 days of one channel. A different month, with different matches, could look different.
  • Correlation isn't cause. Titles, timing, and the event itself all moved together. I can't separate them.
  • The "% watched" figure is my own calculation, not a number YouTube reports.
  • The comparison groups are small. 42 videos were old enough to compare, so single gaps could be noise.

What I'll test next

  • Fewer, higher-stakes uploads on big news days, instead of one video per team. If views per video go up, that supports lead 2.
  • Titles that always carry a number and a consequence, and a note on whether they hold up over the next 28 days.
  • Tracking retention on every Short for a month, so the comparison isn't limited to 42 videos.

How I did this

In YouTube Studio I opened Analytics, switched to Advanced mode, chose the date range (26 October to 23 November 2026) and used Export. The export gave me a per-video table (views, watch time, subscribers, thumbnail impressions, click-through rate) and a daily totals file. I checked that the daily views add up to the 477,128 total, and I read the numbers myself before writing anything. Views follow YouTube's counting, which, for Shorts, counts a view when the video starts playing.

Frequently asked questions

How much of my views came from my top videos?

In the 28 days from 26 October to 22 November 2026, the top 3 of my videos earned about 64% of the channel's views, and the top 10 earned about 81%.

Do old videos still get views on a news-driven Shorts channel?

On my channel, barely. The 412 older videos earned about 3% of the views in this period, with a typical video getting 2 views. This is a result for one news-cycle channel, not a rule for all channels.

How did you measure how much of each Short people watched?

I divided each video's watch time by its views to get average seconds watched, then divided by the video's length. It is my own approximation, not a figure YouTube reports.

Does this prove what makes a Short go viral?

No. It is 28 days of one channel, with small comparison groups, and the titles, the timing and the event all moved together. It shows patterns worth testing, not proof.
