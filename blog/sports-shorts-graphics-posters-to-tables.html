From match posters to ranked tables: how my sports Shorts graphics changed

When I started The Pavilion Report in September 2025, my graphics announced matches. The Shorts that did best later were something else: tables and rankings that answered a question. Here's what changed, how I make these graphics now, and what I check before I publish.

Where I started

I began in early September 2025 and made my graphics in Canva. Most were match cards: two flags, a "VS", the match number, and the time. In the screenshots I collected for this post, the Shorts in that style mostly sit between 18 and 2.1K views.

What changed

Schematic drawings, not my real graphics.

Here is what I see when I compare my early graphics with my most-viewed ones:

  • The early graphics announced an event. They told the viewer a match was happening.
  • The most-viewed ones answer a question or show a comparison: ranked lists, points tables, records. In the screenshots, the five most-viewed Shorts on my Popular tab (332K, 154K, 119K, 117K, and 67K views) are all table or ranking graphics, across cricket and football.
  • Each one has a clear hero. By hero section I mean the most prominent part of the graphic: a picture, a number, or another visual element that catches attention at first glance when it appears in a feed.

This comes from comparing screenshots, not from a controlled test.

How I make them now

  • Tools. I used Canva at the start. When the workload got heavy, I moved to AI tools, mainly ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Lumi Labs.
  • Iteration. I change things step by step: sometimes the colours, sometimes the theme, the text hierarchy, the font sizes, the font placement, or the font colours, depending on what the content needs.
  • Percentages. I decide how much of the frame each element gets: how much space goes to text, how much to a photo, and so on, so the overall visual stays balanced.
  • Images. I usually start from royalty-free images. Whatever you use, make sure you have the right to use it.

Accuracy

I double-check the data against the source. If a wrong figure still ends up in the visual, I ask the generator to make it again. That matters because a graphic is the part of a Short people see first.

What graphics do, and what they don't

In my experience, graphics affect retention and the likes ratio, and the graphics that featured well-known players and their performances or achievements have rewarded me well.

I can't separate the graphic from the topic and the timing. Those table Shorts also covered moments people were already following. I wrote about that in what four real titles taught me about timing.

Three rules for a new creator

1Focus on quality, not quantity.

2Look at your graphic first as a viewer, not as a creator.

3Add your own perspective in the visuals.
